Origin of Man

Traditional Beliefs (Myths and Legends)

Across cultures, origin stories have been handed down through generations to explain how humanity began.

  • The Limba (Sierra Leone)

    They believe that their god, Kanu Masala, descended from the sky, created the first man and woman, and this man and woman gave birth to all humanity near the Wara Wara Mountains before spreading to other areas. The wisest was designated to be their leader.


  • The Yoruba (Nigeria)

    In the beginning, only water existed. The supreme god, Olorun, sent his son named Oduduwa along with 16 chiefs carrying sand. A bird helped scatter the sand to shape hills and valleys. Oduduwa then share the different lands among the chiefs and ruled from the city of Ile-Ife.

Religious Beliefs

Major world religions teach that a divine being created humans.

  • Christianity

    God created the universe in six days. The first man, Adam, was formed from dust, and the first woman, Eve, was made from Adam's rib. They lived in the Garden of Eden.


  • Islam

    Allah created the first man, Adam, and the first woman, Hawwa (Eve). It is said that every human on Earth is descended from them.

Scientific Beliefs

The Theory of Evolution

Some scientists reject divine creation and propose that humans evolved gradually over millions of years from earlier animals, such as primates. In 1859, Charles Darwin introduced this idea after studying plants and animals during his travels. He called his main concept Natural Selection.

The five key points of Darwin's Theory

  • Overproduction

    Organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support.


  • Variation among living things

    No two individuals are exactly alike; each has unique traits.


  • Struggle for survival

    Plants and animals compete for food, space, and safety.


  • Survival of the fittest

    Individuals that best adapted to their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce.


  • Origin of new species

    Over long periods, advantageogus traits are passed down, eventually leading to entirely new species.

Cradle Of Man

Africa: The Birthplace of Humanity

In 1871, Charles Darwin published The Descent of Man, suggesting that humans originated in Africa. Today, scientists widely agree that Africa is the cradle of humankind for two main reasons:

  • The oldest fossils

    The earliest human-like remains have only been found in Africa.


  • Favorable climate

    While much of the world was icy, Africa remained warm and wet, providing ideal conditions for early humans to survive and thrive.

Key Terms

  • Fossils

    The preserved remains of ancient life (e.g., bones or footprints) that have turned into rock.


  • Archaeologist:

    A scientist who studies human history by excavating ancient bones, tools, and ruins (e.g., Louis and Mary Leakey, known for their work in Tanzania).


  • Paleoanthropologist

    A scientist who focuses on ancient human fossils to understand human evolution.


The Five Stages of Human Development

Scientists believe humans evolved in five major stages:

  1. Proconsul (or Australopithecus)

    Lived about 4.2 million years ago in East Africa. They were smaller than modern humans and ate fruits, nuts, roots, and small rodents.


  2. Homo habilis ("Handyman")

    Lived between 3 million and 1.8 million years ago. They had larger brains and were the first to use their thumbs for gripping and toolmaking. A famous 3-million-year-old skeleton from this group, named "Lucy," was found in Ethiopia.


  3. Homo erectus ("Upright Man")

    Lived between 1.5 million and 100,000 years ago. They walked fully upright, used fire, made better tools, and had simple language. They were the first to migrate out of Africa to places like Asia, where fossils such as "Java Man" and "Peking Man" were discovered.


  4. Homo sapiens ("Wise/Thinking Man")

    Lived between 100,000 and 40,000 years ago. They still had ape-like faces with heavy brows but also straight legs and larger brains. Fossils have been found in both Europe and Africa.


  5. Homo sapiens sapiens (Modern Humans)

    This is us. We have flat faces, high foreheads, and smaller brow ridges.

Comparing Humans and Animals

Similarities

  • Primates

    Like monkeys and apes, we share similar limbs and internal organs.


  • Vertebrates

    Like dogs and chimpanzees, we have a backbone.


  • Mammals

    Like cattle, we give live birth and breastfeed our young.

Differences

  • We walk upright on two legs at all times.

  • We have highly advanced brains capable of reasoning and complex problem-solving.

  • We create and use sophisticated tools.

  • We use complex, structured language.

The Human Brain

The brain is like a highly complex computer that controls the entire body. It weighs about 3 pounds (1.5 kg) and contains millions of neurons (specialized nerve cells that send and receive messages).

Three main parts

  1. Cerebrum

    Controls senses and actions (seeing, touching, hearing, moving, thinking, speaking).


  2. Cerebellum

    Controls physical balance to keep us from falling.


  3. Medulla:

    Automatically manages vital organs like the heart and lungs.

The Human Race

Since all humans originated in Africa and later spread across the globe, we all belong to just one race: the human race.

Over thousands of years, people developed different physical traits depending on where they lived, as adaptations to local climates:

  • Negroid (Black people)– Originally from Africa. They developed dark skin to protect against intense sunlight.

  • Caucasoid (White people) – Settled in cooler, less sunny regions. They developed lighter skin to absorb more sunlight.

  • Mongoloid (e.g., Chinese, Japanese, Inuit/Eskimos) – Developed yellowish skin and straight black hair. Those in freezing areas, like the Inuit, evolved short, thick bodies to retain heat.

World Population Trend

Population Growth in Europe

Europe experienced steady population growth from A.D. 1 until the 18th century during the Agricultural and Industrial Revolutions. After these revolutions, growth became very rapid.

Thomas Robert Malthus wrote “An Essay on the Principle of Population” in 1798, arguing that population grows faster than food supply, leading to hunger and poverty

Population Growth in Africa

Some African countries have small populations due to high birth and death rates caused by poor healthcare.

However, many countries experience rapid growth in cities, putting pressure on housing and services.

Factors Affecting Population Growth

  • Climate:

    Climate affects population by influencing food production and living conditions. Extreme weather like droughts or floods can reduce food supply and increase death rates.


  • Disease:

    Diseases reduce population by causing deaths, especially among children and the elderly. Outbreaks can slow population growth.


  • War:

    Wars lead to loss of lives and displacement of people. Although population may grow again after peace returns, war temporarily reduces population size.


  • Food production:

    When food production improves, more people survive and population increases. Poor food supply can lead to hunger and lower population growth.


  • Marriage patterns:

    Early marriage usually leads to more children over a lifetime, increasing population growth. Late marriage often reduces birth rates.

Problems of Rapid Population Growth

  • Unemployment:

    Rapid population growth creates more job seekers than available jobs, leading to many people being unemployed.


  • Low standard of living

    When resources are limited and population is high, people may struggle to afford basic needs like food, housing, and education.


  • Crime increase:Lack of jobs and poverty can push some people into criminal activities, increasing crime rates in society.


  • Prostitution:

    Economic hardship caused by overpopulation may force some individuals into prostitution as a means of survival.
